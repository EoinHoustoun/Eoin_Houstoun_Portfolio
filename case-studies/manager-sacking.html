Does Sacking the Manager Work?

Premier League teams that sack their manager pick up far more points afterwards. Is that the new manager, or would the bounce have happened anyway? 77 sackings across 16 seasons, compared with teams in the same hole that kept their manager.

The trap: teams sack at their lowest point

Clubs rarely sack a manager after a good week. They sack after a bad run, and very often straight after a defeat. Bad runs in football are partly bad luck, and bad luck does not last, so results tend to recover whatever the club does. Statisticians call this regression to the mean. Comparing "before" with "after" credits the new manager with a recovery that was coming anyway.

The fix is a comparison group: teams that hit an equally bad run at the same stage of the season, rated just as highly by the betting market, and did not change manager. If sacked teams improve more than those, that extra is the effect of the sacking.

    What I take from it

    1. The "new manager bounce" is real in the data and mostly not caused by the new manager. Teams in the same position who kept their manager recovered by the same amount.
    2. The betting market already knew. Market expected points barely moved after a sacking, and the sacked teams had been well below what the market expected before it: a sign that part of the bad run was bad luck.
    3. Timing matters for the comparison. Sacked teams averaged about 0.2 points in the game right before the sacking. Only once the controls were matched on that final defeat too did the two groups look identical beforehand.
    4. "No measurable effect" is not "no effect". With 77 sackings the interval rules out a large average boost, but a small one, or a big one for some appointments, is still possible.
